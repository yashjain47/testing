:::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::: SCRUM ::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::  

Helps teams structure and manage their work through a set of rules.

Rules  
-   Every sprint is 4 weeks or less in duration
-   There are no brakes in sprint.
-   Intention of Every sprint is "Potentially shippable" Software.
-   Every sprint includes sprint reviews for stakeholder reviews on the Product.    

Roles
-   Product Owner.
-   Scrum master.
-   Developement team members.

Sprint = A Sprint is a Short, time-boxed period when a scrum team works to complete a set of amount of work.
Sprint duration should be less than 4 weeks.


Scrum 
Origin = Software development
Idealogy = Learn through experiences, self-organize and prioritize, and reflect on wins and losses to continuously improve.
Cadence = Regular, fixed-length sprints (i.e. two weeks)
Practices = Sprint planning, sprint, daily scrum, sprint review, sprint retrospective
Roles = Product owner, scrum master, development team

Kanban
Origin = Lean manufacturing
Idealogy = Use visuals to improve work-in-progress
Cadence = Continuous flow
Practice = Visualize the flow of work, limit work-in-progress, manage flow, incorporate feedback loops
Roles =  No required roles


Neither Scrum nor Kanban is inherently more useful; the most effective choice depends on your project's needs, such as whether you prioritize rapid iterations and structured roles (Scrum) or continuous flow and flexibility for ongoing or unpredictable tasks (Kanban). Scrum is better for projects with fixed delivery cycles and changing requirements, while Kanban excels in environments with continuous work, like maintenance or support, and requires less formal structure. Both are Agile frameworks, and they can even be combined into a hybrid model called Scrumban. 


Choose Scrum when you need:
Structured Sprints and Iterations:
Deliver work in short, fixed-length cycles called sprints. 
Defined Roles and Ceremonies:
Benefit from a structured framework with specific roles (e.g., Scrum Master, Product Owner) and regular meetings. 
Rapid Product Development:
Ideal for software or product development with a clear vision for increments of work. 
Quick Feedback Loops:
Create learning loops by reviewing progress and gathering feedback after each sprint. 


Choose Kanban when you need:
Continuous Flow and Flexibility:
Manage a continuous stream of tasks with less emphasis on fixed timeframes. 
Work-in-Progress (WIP) Limits:
Visualize tasks on a board and limit the amount of work in progress to optimize efficiency. 
Adaptability for Variable Scope:
Adapt to changing priorities on the fly, making it suitable for support or ongoing maintenance. 
Evolutionary Change:
Introduce small, incremental changes to existing processes without major organizational shifts, according to Mountain Goat Software. 


Consider a Hybrid Approach (Scrumban):
For Larger Products:
Large organizations can use Scrum for product development teams and Kanban for supporting teams like DevOps or automation. 
Maximize Benefits:
Combining elements of both methods can help achieve the benefits of both structured iterations and continuous flow.
